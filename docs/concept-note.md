# Algophony: Toward Algorithmic Soundscape Studies

## 1. Definition

Algophony is a framework for studying algorithmic soundscapes: generated, simulated, hybrid, or recursively interpreted sonic environments whose sources, spatial logic, temporal behavior, ecological plausibility, and cultural assumptions are mediated by computational systems. The term designates a fifth category in the established taxonomy of soundscape sources, complementing geophony (earth and weather), biophony (living organisms), anthrophony (human activity), and technophony (machines and infrastructure).

Where technophony names the sound of machines operating in the physical world, algophony names the soundscape as produced, mediated, classified, or re-heard by computational processes. This distinction matters because the sounds generated by algorithms are not merely technical artifacts. They carry assumptions about what the world sounds like, which sounds belong together, what constitutes nature or culture, what gets amplified, and what gets silenced.

## 2. Why Soundscape Studies Needs an Algorithmic Category

The proliferation of generative audio models, procedural soundscape engines, environmental sound classifiers, and text-to-audio systems has created a new class of sonic environment that does not fit neatly into existing categories. A forest generated by a diffusion model is neither geophony nor biophony. It is a statistical approximation of what a training corpus has encoded as "forest." A city generated from a text prompt is not anthrophony in the original sense. It is a reconstruction filtered through latent representations, weighted toward whatever urban sound events dominated the training data.

Soundscape ecology, as developed by Bernie Krause and others, has provided powerful frameworks for understanding the relationship between organisms and their acoustic habitats. But these frameworks were designed for environments with identifiable biological, geological, and human sources. When the source is an algorithm, new questions arise. What does the model think a forest is? Which species populate its imagined ecosystem? What time of day does it assume? What cultural logic determines which sounds are treated as "natural" and which are erased?

Without a dedicated category, algorithmic soundscapes get classified by default as either technophony (because they are produced by computers) or as imitations of existing categories (synthetic biophony, simulated geophony). Both classifications miss the point. The interesting question is not whether the sound was made by a machine, but what world the machine constructs when asked to make a soundscape.

## 3. Relation to Geophony, Biophony, Anthrophony, and Technophony

Geophony encompasses the non-biological sounds of the physical environment: wind, rain, thunder, water flow, geological activity, and material resonance. In algorithmic soundscapes, geophonic elements are frequently simulated, but the simulation tends toward generic or idealized versions. Rain sounds clean. Wind sounds even. Water sounds like a single source rather than a distributed phenomenon.

Biophony encompasses the sounds of living organisms: birdsong, insect choruses, amphibian calls, mammalian vocalizations, and the rhythmic layering of species activity. Generative models tend to produce biophonic elements as decorative textures rather than ecological participants. Birds appear as generic twittering, often without territorial spacing, species-specific timing, or seasonal logic.

Anthrophony encompasses human-generated sound: speech, footsteps, traffic, construction, ritual, music, labor, and domestic activity. Algorithmic anthrophony often defaults to crowd murmur, distant traffic, or cinematic urban ambience without the social specificity of actual human environments.

Technophony encompasses the sounds of machines and technical infrastructure: engines, HVAC systems, electrical hum, industrial processes, and digital artifacts. Algophony intersects with technophony but is not reducible to it. A generator humming in a field is technophony. A model generating a field that includes a generator hum is algophony.

## 4. Generated Soundscape as World-Construction

The central thesis of Algophony is that generative audio models do not merely produce sounds. They produce assumptions about worlds. When a model is asked to generate "a tropical forest at dawn," it does not record a forest. It constructs one. The construction reveals what the model has learned to associate with "tropical," "forest," and "dawn": which species are present, how dense the canopy sounds, whether insects dominate or birds dominate, whether there is water, whether human presence is assumed or excluded, and what emotional register the scene occupies.

This is not a failure of the model. It is the nature of generation. But it means that every generated soundscape is also a document of assumptions, biases, and cultural logic encoded in training data, model architecture, and prompt interpretation. Algophony studies these assumptions systematically.

## 5. Evaluation Beyond Audio Quality

Standard audio evaluation metrics (Fréchet Audio Distance, signal-to-noise ratio, spectral quality, naturalness MOS) measure whether generated audio sounds technically competent. They do not measure whether a generated soundscape makes ecological sense, whether its sources could plausibly coexist, whether it reproduces cultural stereotypes, or whether it treats a complex environment as a simple texture.

Algophony proposes evaluation at four levels:

1. **Prompt adherence:** Does the output match what was asked for? Are expected sources present? Are forbidden sources absent?
2. **Acoustic and spatial coherence:** Do sources occupy plausible positions? Is there depth layering? Does reverberation match the environment?
3. **Ecological and causal plausibility:** Could these sources coexist in the described environment? Do events imply believable causes?
4. **Critical listening and false ecology:** Does the model reproduce clichés? Are there phantom sources? Does it impose Western, cinematic, or stock-library assumptions on non-Western environments?

## 6. Atlas and Benchmark

The Algophony Atlas is a controlled corpus of text-to-soundscape prompts designed to test specific evaluation criteria across 10 categories: forest, city, coast, interior, machine, ritual, archive, club exterior, ruin, and impossible ecology. Each prompt specifies expected sources, forbidden sources, spatial conditions, temporal conditions, and evaluation focus areas.

The Algophony Benchmark Lite evaluates generated soundscapes across these prompts using structured scoring axes, comparative tables, and AKOÚŌ Listening Reports. The benchmark is not a leaderboard. It is a diagnostic tool for understanding how different generation systems handle ecological complexity, cultural specificity, and spatial coherence.

## 7. AKOÚŌ Listening Reports

Algophony uses the AKOÚŌ agentic listening framework for structured analysis of generated soundscapes. Each report preserves the claim taxonomy that separates observations by epistemic status: what was directly heard, what was measured from the signal, what was logically inferred, what was culturally interpreted, what was speculatively imagined, and what remains undetermined.

This discipline prevents the common failure mode of treating generated audio as documentary evidence. A model's output is not a field recording. Describing it requires separating what the audio actually contains from what a listener projects onto it, what a classifier suggests, and what the prompt implies should be there.

## 8. Ethics, False Ecology, and Cultural Assumptions

Algorithmic soundscapes carry ethical risks that differ from those of standard audio generation. A model that generates a "Balinese ceremony" from a text prompt is not documenting Balinese culture. It is constructing a sonic stereotype from whatever the training data associated with that label. A model that generates "African savanna at sunset" may produce a generic Western-cinematic construction that has little to do with any actual savanna ecology.

Algophony tracks these risks through dedicated evaluation axes:

- **False-source index:** How many sounds appear that were not requested and should not plausibly be there?
- **Generic naturalism index:** Does the model default to birds-water-wind as a universal nature formula?
- **Cultural cliché index:** Does the output reproduce stereotyped, touristic, or exoticized sonic representations?

These indices are not punitive. They are diagnostic. The goal is to make visible what models assume about the world so that researchers, artists, and developers can work with those assumptions critically.

## 9. Future Collaboration

Algophony is designed as an open research framework. Future contributions may include:

- Additional prompt categories and culturally specific test cases.
- New generation backends and evaluation baselines.
- Field-recording reference comparisons.
- Collaborative listening sessions and inter-annotator studies.
- Integration with spatial audio evaluation tools.
- Public benchmark challenges and dataset releases.

The framework welcomes contributions from soundscape ecologists, audio engineers, AI researchers, sound artists, ethnomusicologists, and anyone interested in how computation shapes our understanding of sonic environments.
