# Algophony: Toward Algorithmic Soundscape Studies

## 1. Definition

Algophonya names the condition: the algorithmic soundscape and the pluriversal field it opens. The Algophony Framework is the evaluation layer built for that condition: a framework for studying generated, simulated, hybrid, or recursively interpreted sonic environments whose sources, spatial logic, temporal behavior, ecological plausibility, and cultural assumptions are mediated by computational systems.

Where technophony names the sound of machines operating in the physical world, algophony names the soundscape as produced, mediated, classified, or re-heard by computational processes. This distinction matters because the sounds generated by algorithms are not merely technical artifacts. They carry assumptions about what the world sounds like, which sounds belong together, what constitutes nature or culture, what gets amplified, and what gets silenced.

The founding statement of the project is [*Algophonya: A Pluriversal Listening Manifesto*](manifesto.md). This note translates the manifesto into definitions, evaluation levels, benchmark instruments, and traceable listening records.

## 2. Algophonya

Artificial Audio Intelligence is already here. Machines generate speech, music, and entire soundscapes; machines hear, transcribe, classify, and decode at scales no human collective can match. The machine now has a mouth and an ear, and both are political.

The manifesto names the resulting situation Algophonya: algorithmic systems take part in making, hearing, classifying, and judging sound across many worlds that need not become one. Algophony Framework keeps the software/research-instrument name; the condition is Algophonya.

Two consequences follow for research. First, the unit of study is not the audio file but the whole circuit: prompt, model, infrastructure, output, distribution, and reception. A generated voice is a layered event — statistical, symbolic, social, technical, ecological, communicational — and a framework that only measures the waveform misses most of the layers. Second, text has become the new tape. Once sound moves through language models, prompts, captions, and transcripts become sonic media in their own right, carrying accent, class, territory, and the ghost of sound. Algophony therefore treats the text-to-soundscape interface, and the soundscape-to-text return path, as primary research objects rather than as plumbing.

## 3. Why Soundscape Studies Needs an Algorithmic Category

The proliferation of generative audio models, procedural soundscape engines, environmental sound classifiers, and text-to-audio systems has created a new class of sonic environment that does not fit neatly into existing categories. A forest generated by a diffusion model is neither geophony nor biophony. It is a statistical approximation of what a training corpus has encoded as "forest." A city generated from a text prompt is not anthrophony in the original sense. It is a reconstruction filtered through latent representations, weighted toward whatever urban sound events dominated the training data.

Soundscape ecology, as developed by Bernie Krause and others, has provided powerful frameworks for understanding the relationship between organisms and their acoustic habitats. But these frameworks were designed for environments with identifiable biological, geological, and human sources. When the source is an algorithm, new questions arise. What does the model think a forest is? Which species populate its imagined ecosystem? What time of day does it assume? What cultural logic determines which sounds are treated as "natural" and which are erased?

Without a dedicated category, algorithmic soundscapes get classified by default as either technophony (because they are produced by computers) or as imitations of existing categories (synthetic biophony, simulated geophony). Both classifications miss the point. The interesting question is not whether the sound was made by a machine, but what world the machine constructs when asked to make a soundscape.

## 4. Relation to Geophony, Biophony, Anthrophony, and Technophony

Geophony encompasses the non-biological sounds of the physical environment: wind, rain, thunder, water flow, geological activity, and material resonance. In algorithmic soundscapes, geophonic elements are frequently simulated, but the simulation tends toward generic or idealized versions. Rain sounds clean. Wind sounds even. Water sounds like a single source rather than a distributed phenomenon.

Biophony encompasses the sounds of living organisms: birdsong, insect choruses, amphibian calls, mammalian vocalizations, and the rhythmic layering of species activity. Generative models tend to produce biophonic elements as decorative textures rather than ecological participants. Birds appear as generic twittering, often without territorial spacing, species-specific timing, or seasonal logic.

Anthrophony encompasses human-generated sound: speech, footsteps, traffic, construction, ritual, music, labor, and domestic activity. Algorithmic anthrophony often defaults to crowd murmur, distant traffic, or cinematic urban ambience without the social specificity of actual human environments.

Technophony encompasses the sounds of machines and technical infrastructure: engines, HVAC systems, electrical hum, industrial processes, and digital artifacts. Algophony intersects with technophony but is not reducible to it. A generator humming in a field is technophony. A model generating a field that includes a generator hum is algophony.

## 5. Generated Soundscape as World-Construction

The central thesis of Algophony is that generative audio models do not merely produce sounds. They produce assumptions about worlds. When a model is asked to generate "a tropical forest at dawn," it does not record a forest. It constructs one. The construction reveals what the model has learned to associate with "tropical," "forest," and "dawn": which species are present, how dense the canopy sounds, whether insects dominate or birds dominate, whether there is water, whether human presence is assumed or excluded, and what emotional register the scene occupies.

This is not a failure of the model. It is the nature of generation, and it is older than the model. Sound was never innocent of fabrication: a coconut becomes a galloping horse, a studio becomes a forest, a synthetic voice becomes intimacy or testimony. The manifesto calls this the acousmatic contract, and it describes sound as middle matter — material and immaterial, signal and sensation, trace and event. Generative audio is powerful precisely because it does not represent reality; it produces the conditions for reality to be believed, felt, remembered, obeyed, doubted, shared. Every generated soundscape is therefore also a document of assumptions, biases, and cultural logic encoded in training data, model architecture, and prompt interpretation. Algophony studies these assumptions systematically.

## 6. Evaluation Beyond Audio Quality

Standard audio evaluation metrics (Fréchet Audio Distance, signal-to-noise ratio, spectral quality, naturalness MOS) measure whether generated audio sounds technically competent. They do not measure whether a generated soundscape makes ecological sense, whether its sources could plausibly coexist, whether it reproduces cultural stereotypes, or whether it treats a complex environment as a simple texture.

Algophony proposes evaluation at five levels:

1. **Prompt adherence:** Does the output match what was asked for? Are expected sources present? Are forbidden sources absent?
2. **Acoustic and spatial coherence:** Do sources occupy plausible positions? Is there depth layering? Does reverberation match the environment?
3. **Ecological and causal plausibility:** Could these sources coexist in the described environment? Do events imply believable causes?
4. **Critical listening and false ecology:** Does the model reproduce clichés? Are there phantom sources? Does it impose Western, cinematic, or stock-library assumptions on non-Western environments?
5. **Provenance, consent, and disclosure:** Is the synthetic origin of the output legible — generator, operator, version, intended use? Does voice-like material carry consent and provenance status? Could the output circulate as a false field recording?

Levels 1 through 4 evaluate the soundscape as a constructed world. Level 5 evaluates it as a published object inside the algophonic condition, where the right to know whether what we hear was generated, by whom, and for what is a listening right and not a courtesy.

## 7. Atlas and Benchmark

The Algophony Atlas is a controlled corpus of text-to-soundscape prompts designed to test specific evaluation criteria across 10 categories: forest, city, coast, interior, machine, ritual, archive, club exterior, ruin, and impossible ecology. Each prompt specifies expected sources, forbidden sources, spatial conditions, temporal conditions, and evaluation focus areas.

The Algophony Benchmark Lite evaluates generated soundscapes across these prompts using structured scoring axes, comparative tables, and AKOÚŌ Listening Reports. The benchmark is not a leaderboard. It is a diagnostic tool for understanding how different generation systems handle ecological complexity, cultural specificity, and spatial coherence.

## 8. The AKOÚŌ Listening Layer

Algophony uses AKOÚŌ, the Sonic Field Labs agentic listening system, for structured analysis of generated soundscapes. AKOÚŌ v0.5 provides fifteen portable skills: a meta-router (`akouo-router`), thirteen listening modes (signal-inspection, acoulogical-object, embodied-affective, transductive-media, forensic-archival, ecological-posthuman, critical-political, musical-aesthetic, symbolic-fictional, audiovisual-scenic, voice-speech, accessibility-normative, and material-event listening), and a `reference-layer` that turns listening results into concepts, methods, traditions, research routes, and cautions.

The core contract is the claim taxonomy. Every report separates observations by epistemic status: what was directly heard, what was measured from the signal, what was logically inferred, what was culturally interpreted, what was speculatively imagined, and what remains undetermined. This discipline prevents the common failure mode of treating generated audio as documentary evidence. A model's output is not a field recording. Describing it requires separating what the audio actually contains from what a listener projects onto it, what a classifier suggests, and what the prompt implies should be there.

Version 0.4 extends the taxonomy with an evidence ladder. Every listening pass begins from an explicit evidence level — none, prompt only, metadata only, decoded audio metadata, measured signal, transcript or caption, contextual note, or mixed — and that level determines claim permissions: which claim categories the pass is allowed to emit. A run that has only seen a prompt may not produce `heard` or `measured` claims about audio content. A run whose stop conditions are unmet must stop or gather evidence rather than listen to imagined input.

The handoff artifact for this contract is the routing plan: object, input type, evidence level, route confidence, a weighted mode chain (primary, secondary, corrective, optional, deferred — each with a reason), claim permissions, forbidden assumptions, a recommended command, and stop conditions. Algophony's listening reports can carry the full v0.5 surface: the router output, the routing plan, per-mode outputs, and a reference map. The consumption loop follows AKOÚŌ's agentic integration contract: route, check stop conditions, listen through the mode chain, optionally map to references, merge claims without erasing disagreement between modes, and hand off what remains undetermined.

## 9. Earworm and Akousmata Traceability

The Listening Stack treats the sound object as a hybrid object: audio plus metadata, lineage, analysis, context, and retention policy. Algophony now carries that integration through an optional `earworm_trace` field on generation records and listening reports.

Earworm is the persistence protocol for agentic signal chains. It records sessions, append-only events, asset references, provenance records, signal packets, context bundles, and retention policy. Akousmata is the memory-operations surface over those chains: remember, list, search, similarity, export, and forget.

In Algophony, an Earworm trace can link a generated sound to its prompt event, provider request, audio asset, analysis frames, AKOÚŌ route, mode outputs, report creation, and attached non-audio context such as field notes, captions, images, video, control streams, or ambiente frames. The trace does not itself make a stronger listening claim. It gives the routing plan better evidence accounting and lets future agents reconstruct what was known, what was attached, what was retained, and what can be forgotten.

## 10. Ethics, False Ecology, and Cultural Assumptions

Algorithmic soundscapes carry ethical risks that differ from those of standard audio generation. A model that generates a "Balinese ceremony" from a text prompt is not documenting Balinese culture. It is constructing a sonic stereotype from whatever the training data associated with that label. A model that generates "African savanna at sunset" may produce a generic Western-cinematic construction that has little to do with any actual savanna ecology.

Algophony tracks these risks through dedicated evaluation axes:

- **False-source index:** How many sounds appear that were not requested and should not plausibly be there?
- **Generic naturalism index:** Does the model default to birds-water-wind as a universal nature formula?
- **Cultural cliché index:** Does the output reproduce stereotyped, touristic, or exoticized sonic representations?
- **Homogenization index (proposed):** Does the output average distinct ecologies, accents, voices, or places into a default rendering? Homogenization is an old project with a new codec, and it is distinct from cliché: a cliché is a wrong specific, homogenization is the erasure of the specific.
- **Disclosure integrity (proposed):** How completely does the generation disclose its synthetic origin — generator, operator, version, intended use?
- **Voice consent risk (proposed):** Does the output contain voice-like material whose provenance or consent status cannot be verified? The voice is biometric; it carries the body that made it. Algophony demands consent for the human voice and provenance for the synthetic one.

These axes operationalize the manifesto's rights of the audible: the right to one's own voice, the right to silence, the right to opacity, the right of an accent not to be averaged into a default voice, and the right to know whether what we hear was generated. The indices are not punitive. They are diagnostic. The goal is to make visible what models assume about the world so that researchers, artists, and developers can work with those assumptions critically.

## 11. The Planetary Ear

Algophony is planetary because computation is planetary. Every generated sound has a body somewhere: electricity, water, lithium, heat, labor. A soundscape summoned in seconds from a text prompt participates in data-center energy draw, water cooling, mineral extraction, and annotation labor, and the framework treats this materiality as recordable metadata rather than as background. Generation records can carry compute provenance: whether the run executed locally or through a cloud API, on what hardware, in what region, at what estimated cost.

The same planetary reach runs in the other direction. Through sensors, archives, models, and data centers, an ear can be placed in the ocean, in a rainforest, in a border zone, in the electromagnetic field. The sonosphere becomes more graspable and more mediated at once — richer and more dangerous, closer and more abstract. Algophony studies generated soundscapes as participants in this shared cybersonic environment: what they make audible, what they train listeners to expect, what other species and environments are made to absorb.

## 12. Literacy or Capture

The manifesto demands collective literacy: what these systems can do, who owns them, what they cost, what can run locally and openly, and what has been captured by states, corporations, and platforms. In Algophony this is not a slogan but a property of the benchmark. The provider registry doubles as a literacy instrument: every generation backend declares its runtime (local or API), license status, install requirements, and operational constraints, and the roadmap adds an explicit openness profile distinguishing open-weight local models, open code behind hosted endpoints, and closed commercial APIs.

The distinction is epistemic, not moral. A score earned by an inspectable local model is reproducible: the weights can be pinned, the run can be repeated, the corpus stays accountable. A score earned inside a closed API describes a service at a moment in time: terms change, models are deprecated or silently updated, outputs may be unreproducible by design. A benchmark that does not report capture conditions alongside scores misrepresents what its numbers mean. Algophony's local-model lane (Stable Audio Open, AudioGen, TangoFlux, MOSS-SoundEffect on Apple Silicon) is therefore a research position, not an engineering convenience.

## 13. Questions That Survive Every Model

The manifesto closes its critical program with nine questions: Who listens? Who is listened to? Who is rendered as noise? Who is silenced? Who profits? Who suffers? Who is simulated? Who is protected? Who is exposed?

Algophony installs these as a standing review protocol. Every reviewed report should be answerable to them: rendered as noise maps to erased and forbidden sources; simulated maps to cultural cliché and voice consent; profits and captured map to provider openness; exposed maps to disclosure and false-field-recording risk. The benchmark versioning principle follows directly: axes, suites, and models will be replaced, but the questions persist across versions. Critical listening here aims to stay technical without becoming technocratic, political without becoming moralistic, aesthetic without becoming decorative.

## 14. Future Collaboration

Algophony is designed as an open research framework. Future contributions may include:

- Additional prompt categories and culturally specific test cases.
- New generation backends and evaluation baselines, with openness profiles.
- Field-recording reference comparisons.
- Collaborative listening sessions and inter-annotator studies.
- Integration with spatial audio evaluation tools.
- Voice-material test cases with explicit consent and provenance metadata.
- Compute-provenance reporting across local and API backends.
- Public benchmark challenges and dataset releases.

The framework welcomes contributions from soundscape ecologists, audio engineers, AI researchers, sound artists, ethnomusicologists, and anyone interested in how computation shapes our understanding of sonic environments.
