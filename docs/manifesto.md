# Algophonya: A Pluriversal Listening Manifesto

*A call for awareness, invention, refusal, and repair in the age of machine sound. Written in the name of all ears — and of all the other ways listening happens. Read it aloud if you can, and bend it to your own ground.*

> Founding statement of the Algophony Framework. The concept note
> (`concept-note.md`) translates this manifesto into definitions, evaluation
> levels, and benchmark instruments. The manifesto is the source; the framework
> is one way of keeping its questions operational.

## I. The Machines Speak, and They Hear

Listen. Wherever you are, something nearby can probably hear you. The phone, the speaker, the television, the car: each carries an ear that never tires and a mouth that never runs out of breath.

Machines now generate speech, music, storms, cities, whole sonic worlds. And they listen. They separate one voice from a crowd, name a language among hundreds, follow the snap of a shrimp through a coral reef, register the groan of melting ice. They can also fabricate any of these sounds from no event at all.

Most of us meet this condition daily, in small ways. A playlist assembled by a system that studies what keeps us from leaving. A song by no one, made to be counted rather than heard. A dead singer released on a new record. A gentle voice in the kitchen that answers our children, and remembers them.

This is wonder and danger in the same packet. The tool that restores a grandmother's voice can clone it without her. The system that hears a disappearing species can also hear a protest, or a bedroom. The agent that speaks gently may carry a policy, a market, or a state inside its voice; its personality is not a soul but a disposition someone installed. And before any machine speaks with authority, it has already listened: to archives, to users, to the unequally recorded world. Neither its mouth nor its ear is neutral.

## II. This Is Algophonya

Call the condition **Algophonya**: the algorithmic soundscape, in which computation takes part in making, hearing, classifying, and judging the world's sound — and the plural field that soundscape opens, where listening is shared, unequally and sometimes incompatibly, across bodies, machines, species, and worlds. Not one global ear. A mesh of partial, situated ways of hearing.

It did not begin with artificial intelligence. The recording machine made the past audible; the loudspeaker made the absent present; radio folded distance; tape made sound cuttable; sampling made the archive playable; synthesis made the impossible audible; networks made listening planetary. Generative systems add the strangest step: the named thing appears. A voice without a throat. A storm without weather.

The danger is not the new sound. It is the old dream inside it. The colonial project was to make many worlds answer to one measure: one language, one map, one archive, one ear. That dream now compresses into models and defaults. A language becomes "low-resource." An accent becomes an error. A ceremony becomes content. A forest becomes training data.

Algophonya begins from the opposite premise. There are many worlds, and they do not need to become one in order to coexist. Aymara weavers have a word for the gray made of black and white threads set side by side, so close they shimmer, neither dissolving into the other: ch'ixi. The algorithmic soundscape can be ch'ixi too — archive and hallucination, watershed and server, prompt and prayer, held together in friction, each keeping its difference. We do not ask for a universal ear. We ask for many ears, many silences, many thresholds, and many ways to listen back.

## III. The Synthesizer Was Never Innocent

A century ago, men in love with engines promised an art of noises. We have it. The machines compose now, and the old question arrives with new force: whose machines, whose noise?

The laboratory that fits in a laptop today once belonged to states, militaries, and broadcasters. The lineage that gave us new instruments and impossible music also gave us acoustic weapons, surveillance microphones, and the oscillator turned toward pain. One machine, two roots.

But the machine never stays where power puts it. Cheap, copied, circuit-bent, patched into the wrong hands, it has always made musics the laboratory never authorized: sound systems wired from salvage, genres born at the periphery from broken gear and borrowed bandwidth, voices amplified against the systems built to contain them. People take the imposed instrument and make it say something else. That refusal-by-use is where we begin.

## IV. Sound Becomes Data

Once sound becomes data — transcript, spectrogram, token, weight — models do to it what they do to everything: compare, compress, classify, predict, and dream errors into it. Then the answer returns as sound: a voice that can perform tenderness, hold a silence, command, or confess.

But sound is never only information. A transcript drops the grain of the throat, the breath, the social risk inside the utterance. An embedding can make a voice searchable while stripping away the terms by which that voice understands itself. And models lean toward the languages and accents that already rule the servers; tongues with less data are heard as noise, or not heard at all. The machines also inherited our oldest habit of attention: seize the voice, file the world as background.

So listen down as well as across. Beneath perception, sound now runs as samples, frames, and vectors, at grains and speeds no ear resolves. Beneath the files runs the stack: microphone, codec, dataset, model, chip, mineral, water, energy, labor, law. There is no pure digital sound floating free of matter; the most abstract audio model reaches the earth through mines and cooling water. The cloud has a body, and the body is rarely where its owners live. The synthetic voice carries hidden work: recordists and annotators, moderators and miners, and the users whose traces become training. Neither of the two easy stories survives this view. Artificial intelligence is not magic arriving from nowhere, and it is not a neutral tool. It is humanmade and world-made, a laboring assemblage that hears through the bodies, histories, and defaults that built it.

An ecological politics of algorithmic audio starts here. Local models where scale is unnecessary. Open systems where audit is a public need. Closed protocols where protection requires limits. Community ownership where communities are the source. Count the water before deployment. The question is not only who owns the model; it is who owns the conditions that allow the model to hear.

## V. We Do Not Reject the Synthetic

We do not condemn generated voices, fabricated rooms, synthetic instruments, or cloned timbres. Artificial audio will not be un-invented, and refusal alone is not a strategy. But we do not accept the sales pitch either, where every ear is a market and every voice a product waiting to happen. Between the pitch and the funeral there is a third position: the listener's.

The political act is not purity. It is intention: consent, context, relation, repair. Whoever decides the use decides much of the politics.

This is possible because sound is middle matter — pressure and meaning at once, a wave you can measure and a memory you cannot. A coconut becomes a galloping horse; a studio becomes a forest; a synthesized voice becomes intimacy, or command. So simulation is not only deception. It is rehearsal, prosthesis, and mirror, and a mirror can be a critique. The question is not whether a synthetic sound is real. It is what it bends, what it erases, what it exposes — who controls the mirror, and who must live inside its reflection.

And there is joy here. Say it plainly, because the warnings are true and still not the whole truth: instruments no orchestra imagined, rooms rebuilt around a deaf ear, a ruined tape sung back to life, compositions at scales no unaided ear could hold. An algorithm is not owned by capture, either. It can be a score, a ritual procedure, a community archive, a way of composing relations at scales the ear cannot hold alone. People are already making things inside this condition that nobody sold them and nobody predicted. This manifesto is for them too.

## VI. The Ear Must Expand

Listening does not belong only to ears. It happens through skin and bone, caption and gesture, memory and architecture; in bodies that hear, bodies that do not, bodies that hear differently, and systems that sense without living. To write in the name of all ears is poetic, not anatomical.

No ear is the master ear. A human hears a song as grief, dance, or prayer. A machine registers spectrum, embedding, probability. A whale, a glacier, a cochlear implant, and a server farm each open a different audibility. None exhausts listening; none is innocent. Ask whether a machine can feel music and the question comes back the wrong size: a machine can model the signs by which feeling becomes legible, and recognition is not understanding. Neither mode exhausts the song. The practice is not to crown one ear and retire the rest, but to compose relations among them without erasing their difference.

Call that practice **algorithmic acoulogy**: the study and composition of listening across bodies, sensors, models, habitats, and institutions. It asks how sound becomes signal, signal becomes model, model becomes decision, and decision returns to the world as action — and where, in that chain, listening can be contested, redirected, or repaired.

To hear this way is to hear at several scales at once. A river's sound is water now, rain before, extraction upstream, drought ahead, law around it, and species within it. A voice is breath now, body before, lineage within, and perhaps a synthetic double beyond death. Not everything can be formalized. Not everything should be optimized. Not everything audible should be made available.

## VII. No Machine Hearing Is Final

A machine's hearing is a claim, not a verdict. No transcription, biometric match, species count, emotion score, or authenticity check should become sovereign. Every listening system must remain contestable: every classification with a path of appeal, every automated decision with a responsible name attached, every output with the possibility of correction.

The system will fail. The only question is whether its failure has been designed as tyranny or as repair. A listening machine can mishear an accent, mistranscribe a name, read distress as aggression, confuse a ritual with noise. When such systems enter courts, borders, hospitals, and archives, their mistakes harden into administrative reality.

So we ask of every machine that hears, and of everyone who speaks for one, an honest grammar of claims. Say what was heard. Mark what was measured. Separate what was inferred from what was interpreted. Confess what is speculation. Leave room for what remains undetermined. A hearing that cannot tell these apart is not listening; it is asserting.

Machine listening is strong once the question is set: find this species, separate this voice, transcribe this speech. Ecological and political listening begins earlier, with questions no model can answer from inside its own frame. Should this be recorded at all? Who has the right to hear it? When is classification itself the violence? When should the microphone be turned off? A machine may assist listening. It may not close it.

## VIII. The Voice Is a Body

A few seconds of audio can now imitate a voice. And a voice is not a sound like any other. It is biometric: it carries the body that made it — age, health, region, class, fatigue, fear — or invites guesses about them. Lifted from that body, it can be puppeted: the dead made to testify, a confession invented, a singer replaced, a child calling a mother from a number she has never seen.

The archives that trained these systems are thick with voices that never agreed: singers, callers, broadcasters, the recorded dead, the unpaid and uncredited. And some voices were never individual property at all. They are communal, ceremonial, ancestral, or place-bound, and permission for them cannot be reduced to one person's checkbox.

The same power heals. It can return speech to someone illness silenced, let a language's last speaker keep teaching after they are gone, lift a grandmother's song from a ruined tape. The capacity that violates is the capacity that restores; consent is the beginning of the difference, not its end.

So: consent for the human voice, provenance for the synthetic one. And one question asked every time: whose voice does the machine call neutral? When a million voices are averaged into one default, someone's accent becomes the human standard and everyone else's becomes deviation. That decision is as old as empire.

## IX. Rights of the Audible

When everything spoken can be caught, transcribed, cloned, scored, and simulated, speech needs a new economy and silence needs a defense. So we claim, for speakers, listeners, and the more-than-human worlds drawn into our microphones:

- the right to one's own voice, against its cloning, sale, coercive use, and unauthorized resurrection;
- the right to silence: to go unrecorded, unprofiled, and not become data;
- the right to opacity: to speak, sing, mask, or withdraw without being forcibly decoded;
- the right of an accent, a dialect, a way of listening, to exist without being averaged away;
- the right to know whether a sound was generated, cloned, or captured, by whom, and to what end;
- the right to consent, refusal, revocation, credit, and payment when voices and sounds enter datasets, models, or products;
- the right of communities to their own protocols for sound that is ancestral, sacred, endangered, or place-bound;
- the right to contest any machine hearing that touches a life, and to redress, with an answer and a name, when it causes harm;
- the right to remake the tools.

We claim these rights late, and we say so. Our voices are already in the archives; some are already inside weights that no one can open, audit, or empty — taken without asking, kept without record, unverifiable even to the takers. The commons of the audible has been enclosed before; it is being enclosed again, as training data. None of this embarrasses the claim. It is where the claim comes from: rights are always named late, by the already-dispossessed, from inside the loss. So these rights face two ways at once — forward, as the defaults of everything still to be built, and backward, as the measure by which what was already done can be judged, disputed, and repaired. To claim a right after the taking is not a fantasy of restoration. It is the refusal to let the accomplished fact write the law.

And to those who build and deploy: consent before capture; provenance before circulation; labeling before belief; audit before deployment; accounting before scale; compensation before extraction. Refusal, deletion, and revocation are design requirements, not features. And measure what matters: a system that produces a convincing fake has not passed a listening test. It has passed a deception test.

Homogenization is an old colonial project with a new codec. To keep the world's accents is to keep its many ways of knowing.

## X. The Planetary Ear, and the Times to Come

An ear can now be placed almost anywhere: the deep ocean, a rainforest canopy, the coolant hum of a data center, the crackle of the magnetosphere. It can arrive with company: instruments that remember the light, the air, and the weather in which a sound occurred. From a chair on one continent you can hear a war on another, a melting glacier, a dying language, rain on a roof a hemisphere away. Horror and wonder arrive down the same wire. Whether that reach becomes care or control depends on why the ear was placed, who maintains it, who profits, and who is allowed to listen back. The wager is that machines can help us hear what industrial habit taught us to ignore — damaged reefs, stressed forests, endangered languages — without turning everything they touch into property.

And beware the visible ear. The friendly voice, the glowing waveform, the generated song: these are the interface. The real listening may be elsewhere: in the dataset, the moderation queue, the biometric template, the copyright filter, the border database. The listening system is rarely where the interface says it is.

We are the most listened-to generation that has ever lived, and we may be the one that listens least. Machine listening will keep deepening; ours must deepen faster.

The archive of the future is being recorded now. Machines are already training on machine sound; a future that cannot tell its captures from its fabrications inherits a poisoned well. Provenance is not paperwork. It is a gift to listeners not yet born — to the historians of what our world sounded like, and to whatever they will build to hear it again.

The Earth, meanwhile, is not silent matter awaiting our interpretation. It vibrates, resonates, warns, and exceeds us. To listen well is to listen past the human: to the living and the geological, the dead and the not-yet-born, and to the conditions that allow a sound to be heard at all.

## XI. Put This Down

For one minute, stop reading and listen to wherever you are. Count the sounds nobody made on purpose. Count the machines. Ask which of them can hear you back. That minute is the whole method of this manifesto, performed.

Then, if you want more:

- Follow one sound to its infrastructure. Find the data center, the antenna, or the substation nearest your home, and go listen to it.
- Find where your voice assistant keeps its recordings, and listen to yourself as data.
- Play a generated song next to a recorded one. Listen for what the average left out.
- Learn one sound of your place that is disappearing — a bird, a bell, a language — and record it with consent, or let it stay unrecorded on purpose.
- Keep one sound in your life off the record. Know which one it is.
- When a machine tells you about the world, sort the claim: heard, measured, inferred, or guessed.

None of this requires a laboratory. Listening is the cheapest instrument we have, and the hardest to automate well.

## XII. Questions That Survive Every Model

We need literacy before opinions: what these systems are, what they cost, whose data they ingest, how they fail. Critical listening stays technical without turning technocratic, political without turning doctrinal, tender without turning naive. Whatever the systems become, keep asking:

Who listens, and who is listened to?

Who is rendered as signal, and who as noise?

Whose accent is the default, whose an error, whose a suspicion?

Whose languages never entered the data, and so go missing from the future?

Whose grief, labor, ceremony, or habitat becomes training material?

Who profits, who is exposed, who is simulated without consent?

Who owns the interface, and who owns what runs beneath it?

Who can audit the model, refuse the capture, delete the trace, repair the harm?

What is accuracy, and what is justice?

And when a machine hearing hurts someone, who answers, with a name?

Models will be replaced, this year and every year after. These questions will not.

## XIII. To Listen Is to Give Space

No single discipline can hear all of this, and that is the point. Sound has always slipped between fields — physics and politics, engineering and prayer — and the practice of listening must cross with it. To listen well now is to hear a sound, in the same instant, as wave and wound, memory and market, model and habitat.

To listen is to give space: to let another being, signal, memory, species, machine, or world take its time without being captured the moment it appears. We listen not to purify sound but to understand it; not to flee into a clean past but to build a fairer, stranger future; not to make machines human but to learn what other forms of listening can do.

The tools are real, and they cannot be left to those who sell the future back to us as inevitability. A tool you cannot open is a tool that is opening you. What is hackable is where freedom still lives. So take the tools. Inspect them, slow them, break them, remake them, teach with them, compose with them. Listen through them, and listen against them.

To listen as study. To listen as care. To listen as resistance. To listen as world-making. To listen more, and to listen again.

We write in the name of all ears — and of all the other ways listening happens.

## Conceptual Notes

**Algophonya.** The algorithmic soundscape and the pluriversal field it opens: the condition in which computational systems take part in making, hearing, classifying, and judging sound, across many worlds that need not become one.

**Algorithmic acoulogy.** The study and practice of how listening is organized, and how it could be reorganized: across bodies, machines, habitats, datasets, institutions, and infrastructures.

**Pluriverse.** A world of many worlds; the refusal to let machine listening become a single, homogenizing global ear.

**Ch'ixi.** An Aymara concept carried into theory by Silvia Rivera Cusicanqui: the motley quality of a thing woven from opposites — black and white threads reading as gray from a distance — coexisting in friction, never fusing. Here, a way to inhabit the algorithmic soundscape without surrendering to either purity or synthesis.

**The stack.** The material chain beneath digital sound: microphone, codec, dataset, model, chip, mineral, water, energy, labor, law.

**The ladder of claims.** An honest grammar for reporting any act of hearing, machine or human: heard, measured, inferred, interpreted, speculated, undetermined. Each rung licenses a different kind of speech.

## Lineage

This manifesto does not begin from zero. It stands in the long conversation of acoustic ecology and soundscape thought; of deep listening as a discipline of attention; of musique concrète, radiophonic workshops, and the tape-splicers of Cairo and Paris; of dub and the sound systems built from salvage; of disability listening, environmental justice, media archaeology, critical data studies, and the study of machine listening; and of decolonial and pluriversal thought from Abya Yala, where the Zapatista invitation still stands: a world where many worlds fit.

Some debts carry names. Luigi Russolo, who promised an art of noises. Halim El-Dabh and Pierre Schaeffer, who taught the recording to become an instrument. Daphne Oram and Delia Derbyshire, who built new voices from oscillators and tape. R. Murray Schafer, who named the soundscape. Pauline Oliveros, who made listening a lifelong practice. Jacques Attali, who heard the future arriving in noise. Édouard Glissant, from whom we take the right to opacity. Donna Haraway, for knowledge that knows where it stands. Arturo Escobar, for the pluriverse. Silvia Rivera Cusicanqui, for ch'ixi. Most debts carry no name: the listeners, archivists, communities, and more-than-human worlds that opened this ground first.

The task is not to claim a new beginning, but to listen again from inside the algorithmic condition.
